\section{Introduction}
\label{sect:intro}

Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) PET/CT is now routinely used for staging prostate cancer, for planning and following radioligand therapy, and for assessing response~\cite{ref_psma_clinical}.
Longitudinal imaging provides a baseline examination and one or more follow-up examinations.
Registering each follow-up to the baseline makes it possible to compare tumor burden voxel by voxel, track individual lesions, and accumulate dose~\cite{ref_psmareg_challenge}.
For these uses, a registration must do two things at once: it has to align the anatomy, and it must not itself change the PET-derived quantities that are compared across time points.
Two such quantities are the metabolic tumor volume (MTV) and the total lesion glycolysis (TLG).
A deformation that compresses or stretches a lesion changes both, so a change measured after registration can be an artifact of the registration rather than of the disease.
The Learn2Reg 2026 PSMAReg challenge~\cite{ref_learn2reg_hansen,ref_learn2reg_alessa,ref_psmaregdata} formalizes this setting: each follow-up scan is warped to its baseline by a dense displacement field, which is scored on organ overlap, boundary distance, folding, and the change of MTV and TLG caused by the warp.
The two goals can compete: limiting deformation may protect lesion measurements while leaving the anatomy misaligned, whereas improving alignment may distort the lesions being measured.

Classical registration solves an optimization problem per image pair~\cite{ref_demons,ref_syn,bspline}; this is accurate but slow for whole-body scans.
Learning-based registration replaces the per-pair optimization with one forward pass of a trained network~\cite{ref_voxelmorph_cvpr,ref_svf_voxelmorph,ref_transmorph}, and instance optimization (IO) can refine the network output per pair at test time.
The coarse-to-fine Laplacian pyramid network LapIRN~\cite{ref_lapirn} recovers the large deformations between whole-body scans while keeping the transformation smooth and invertible.
With a network and optional IO, preservation constraints can act during training, during per-pair refinement, or at both stages; the choice affects both registration quality and runtime.
The label-guided IO studied here uses predicted organ and lesion masks for each pair; we evaluate its output against the provided labels.
Its PET-preservation losses act on the predicted lesion mask, so a lesion absent from that mask receives no direct preservation constraint.

Our earlier Learn2Reg submission introduced the affine--LapIRN--IO pipeline with PET-preservation and per-bone rigidity losses and evaluated its selected configurations on challenge validation cases using surrogate labels~\cite{ref_koegl_arxiv}.
In this extension, we retrain on a new patient-level split of the released labeled cohort, reserve 28 patients for final evaluation, and add controlled loss-placement comparisons, tuned baselines, patient-level statistics, and per-lesion analyses.
These 28 patients were among the challenge data available during development of the earlier method, so the split provides a holdout for the present analyses rather than an independent external cohort.

First, we ask whether the PET-preservation and bone-rigidity losses reduce registration-induced change and what alignment accuracy they cost, both with and without IO.
We then ask whether applying the losses only during IO can recover the preservation obtained by also using them in training, and compare that choice with the faster network-only setting.
Finally, we analyze changes to individual lesions and the coverage of the predicted lesion masks used by IO to understand what limits preservation.
Because many PSMA lesions lie in bone, we also compare rigidity penalties for low-resolution whole-body CT, where thin skeletal structures make local constraints difficult to apply.
Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a controlled comparison of where PET-preservation and rigidity losses act (network training, IO, or both), showing that with the losses, IO improves alignment without measurably increasing the change in lesion volume and uptake, and that applying them only in IO is the more accurate placement;
  \item an analysis of the remaining change, showing that lesions missed by the predicted masks dominate it and that scan-level MTV and TLG hide per-lesion change, which neither provided masks nor per-lesion losses removed;
  \item a comparison of rigidity penalties for whole-body CT, showing that a per-bone rigid fit avoids the boundary leakage of finite-difference penalties and the loss of skeleton under erosion, and preserves within-bone distances best;
  \item a patient-level evaluation with the official challenge scorer, a selection rule that weighs preservation against a fixed Dice tolerance, and tuned NiftyReg and ConvexAdam baselines.
\end{itemize}

\caption{\label{fig:pipeline}
Registration pipeline.
The follow-up (moving) and baseline (fixed) PET/CT scans are aligned by an affine pre-registration, then by the LapIRN network, which predicts a dense deformation.
Instance optimization (IO) refines this deformation per pair before the moving scan is resampled into the fixed frame.
Network training and IO use the same groups of loss terms: registration accuracy, bone rigidity, PET quantification, and deformation regularization (Sec.~\ref{sect:objective}).
The bone-rigidity and PET-quantification terms can be used in training, in IO, or in both; our method uses them only in IO.}
