\section{Discussion}
\label{sect:discussion}

The central trade-off is between anatomical alignment and registration-induced change to lesion measurements.
PET-preservation losses reduced volume and uptake change at a cost of about two to three Dice points, while the rigidity loss reduced volume change at a smaller alignment cost.
When IO was used, applying the losses only during refinement improved alignment over using them in both training and IO.
The preservation differences were not statistically significant, but their confidence intervals do not establish that the placements are equivalent.
The residual PET change was concentrated in lesions that the predicted masks used by IO missed, showing that the choice of loss placement is only part of the preservation problem.

\subsection{Choosing Where to Apply the Losses}
Which placement to choose depends on the time budget and on the availability of predicted organ and lesion masks for IO.
If IO is affordable, about 2.5 minutes per pair on our GPU, the network can be trained for accuracy alone and the losses applied during refinement.
Compared with training and IO with both loss groups, this gained 1.7 Dice points.
The paired intervals still allow worse MTV or TLG preservation with IO-only placement, so the result supports an accuracy gain but leaves the preservation trade-off uncertain.
Without IO, the network alone registers a pair in 11~s, and training with the losses reduced registration-induced change to lesion volume and uptake.
The training losses are therefore not superseded by IO, but they serve a different setting, e.g.\ fast registration of many follow-up examinations.

One possible explanation for the IO-only result is how each stage locates lesions.
During network training, lesion labels define the preservation losses, but the trained registration network receives only images at inference and must respond to lesion-related image cues without a mask.
IO instead uses an explicit mask from a separately trained lesion-segmentation model, which can direct preservation to the predicted lesions in each pair.
This could explain why adding the preservation losses during training provides little further measured preservation once IO is applied.
The comparison does not test what lesion information the registration network learns, and the segmentation model cannot by itself explain the Dice advantage of IO-only placement: both IO variants use the same predicted masks.

\subsection{Limits of Lesion Preservation}
On the predicted lesion masks that IO optimises, the PET losses removed almost all volume change (from 8.1\,\% to 1.0\,\%), and weights ten times higher than the tuned ones changed none of the metrics.
The remaining change on the ground-truth lesions is therefore not a matter of the optimisation or the weights but of the masks: lesions that nnU-Net missed cannot be preserved and contributed most of the remaining volume and uptake change, although they make up only 6\,\% of the lesion volume.
Their weight comes from patients with little tumour volume: the metric is relative per scan and averaged over patients, and in the third of patients with the smallest lesion volume, missed lesions made up 45\,\% of the volume (18\,\% and 6\,\% in the other thirds) and caused 70\,\% of the missed-lesion contribution.
Detecting more lesions at the price of precision is no remedy: on the validation set, IO with a recall-oriented mask (SUV threshold minus physiological organs, combined with nnU-Net), which detected more lesions but had a precision of 0.18, preserved worse than without the PET losses, because preserving the false-positive regions came at the expense of the true lesions.
Better lesion segmentation, rather than a different loss weighting, is therefore the most promising way to improve preservation in IO.
IO with the ground-truth lesion masks supports this: it lowered the volume change of the ground-truth lesions from 7.6\,\% to 2.1\,\% and the uptake change from 8.7\,\% to 3.2\,\%, close to the resampling floor, and was slightly more accurate; the contributions of both missed and detected lesions shrank.

Because IO meets its objective on the predicted masks already at small weights, the PET weight mainly trades accuracy.
A factor of $10^{-4}$ of the training-derived weights gave most of the preservation for 0.1 Dice points, whereas the factor chosen by the tuning on the validation set ($\times0.1$) cost 1.6 points more for 0.4 points less volume change; the tuning grid contained no factor between 0 and $\times0.1$.
Even with the ground-truth masks, a ten times larger weight did not improve preservation.

In IO, the two Jacobian terms carried the volume preservation, whereas the volume term $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MTV}}$ cost 1.3 Dice points and contributed no significant preservation, and the uptake term $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TLG}}$ had no significant effect.
A smaller set of terms would likely suffice; we kept all four because they were chosen before the test set was evaluated, and all effects of single terms were below one percentage point.

Aggregate MTV and TLG changes hide how individual lesions are deformed.
Small lesions changed most in every condition, partly because a change of one or two voxels is already a large relative change.
Without the losses, IO increased the distortion of lesions, most of all of small bone-associated lesions, and bone lesions tended to grow.
The rigidity loss acted only on bone-associated lesions, as intended, and the PET losses on both bone and soft-tissue lesions; with both groups of losses, bone lesions changed least, and their systematic growth disappeared.

\subsection{Interpreting the Evaluation}
The significance-based ranking of the challenge was decided mainly by accuracy: differences in alignment between methods are consistent across patients and reach significance, whereas differences in MTV and TLG vary strongly between patients and rarely do.
On the validation set, the ranking consequently chose our variant without any of the losses.
The ranking also rewards methods that hardly deform: IO directly after the affine pre-registration had the lowest MTV and TLG change of all methods and ranked fifth with 52.7\,\% Dice, above our network without IO with 74.8\,\%.
Preservation metrics should therefore be read together with accuracy, not on their own.
A further effect of the scorer is that warping the PET image changes TLG even for the identity transformation (2.9\,\% on average); this floor applies equally to all methods but limits how small a TLG change can be reported.
Per-lesion changes, which do not cancel between lesions, would complement the aggregate metrics.

Both the accuracy and the preservation of IO are bounded by the labels it uses.
On the predicted organ labels, IO raised Dice from 79.0\,\% to 85.9\,\%, on the ground-truth labels only from 74.8\,\% to 77.5\,\%; for lesions, it preserved the predicted masks almost perfectly but not the missed lesions.
Within a challenge whose metrics are computed on segmentations, better segmentation is thus a direct route to better registration scores, for organs as well as for lesions.
Outside this setting, label overlap and the change of MTV and TLG are proxies: what matters clinically is whether registration supports downstream tasks such as lesion tracking, response assessment, or dose accumulation.
Future work should therefore evaluate registration on such tasks and clinical endpoints rather than optimise further for segmentation-based metrics.

The baseline comparison also illustrates the role of deformation regularity.
NiftyReg, tuned on the validation set where it rarely folded, exceeded the folding threshold on 21\,\% of the test pairs, with three pairs above 0.8\,\%.
The ranking selected its least-regularised setting because accuracy separated the settings more than folding; a setting chosen on the validation set did not transfer to new patients.
Our networks with and without IO stayed below the threshold on every pair.

\subsection{Limitations}
The study uses a single cohort with 28 test patients, so small differences, in particular in MTV and TLG, could not be resolved, and generalisation to other scanners and tracers is untested.
The images were resampled to about $2.7\times2.7\times3.3$~mm, at which many lesions consist of few voxels and small lesions are dominated by discretisation.
The organ labels used for evaluation are themselves automatic (TotalSegmentator), and the lesion labels used by IO are predictions with a Dice of about 0.5, which, as shown above, limits preservation in IO.
All patients, including the test patients, were part of the challenge data with which the earlier version of the method was developed; the test patients were not used for any decision in the present study.
We studied one network architecture, and we measured the change caused by registration, not its effect on clinical decisions such as response assessment.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sect:conclusion}

Lesion-preserving losses reduce the change of PET measurements caused by deformable registration of longitudinal whole-body PSMA PET/CT, at a cost in anatomical alignment.
When per-pair instance optimisation is feasible and predicted lesion masks can be obtained, applying the losses only during refinement yielded higher alignment accuracy than using them in training as well.
The present test set did not resolve whether PET preservation differs between these placements.
Without instance optimisation, the training losses reduced the change in lesion measurements.
The remaining error was largest for lesions missed by the predicted masks used in instance optimisation, indicating that mask coverage limits the benefit of stronger preservation constraints.
