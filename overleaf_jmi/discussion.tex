\section{Discussion}
\label{sect:discussion}

The main result is a bounded practical choice: loss-guided IO improves alignment over network output when predicted masks and per-pair refinement time are available, but the present data do not establish better or equivalent image-level PET preservation.
Applying the losses only during IO gives higher alignment accuracy than also using them in training, with an unresolved preservation trade-off.
The remaining PET change reflects three limits of the method and evaluation: incomplete lesion masks, cancellation between lesions in image totals, and a mismatch between the optimized and evaluated quantities.
Bone rigidity serves both as a PET-preservation strategy and as a constraint on within-bone geometry: rigidity alone reduced particular PET endpoints, but it provided no detected additional preservation benefit on top of the PET losses.

\subsection{Choosing Where to Apply the Losses}
Network-only registration takes about 11~s per pair on our GPU and requires only the image pair at inference.
Loss-guided IO additionally requires predicted organ and lesion masks and about 2.5~minutes per pair.
Its advantage in this comparison is additional alignment rather than demonstrated improvement in MTV or TLG preservation.
The network output remains the practical choice when masks or per-pair runtime are unavailable.

Among the IO variants, applying the losses only during refinement supports an accuracy gain, but the paired intervals still allow worse MTV or TLG preservation than when the losses are also used in training.
Without IO, training with the losses lowered the mean registration-induced changes in lesion volume and uptake; only the MTV reduction was detected after correction.
Training with the losses may therefore remain relevant when many follow-up examinations must be registered without per-pair refinement.

One possible explanation for the IO-only result is how each stage locates lesions.
During network training, lesion labels define the preservation losses, but the trained registration network receives only images at inference and must respond to lesion-related image cues without a mask.
IO instead uses an explicit mask from a separately trained lesion-segmentation model, which can direct preservation to the predicted lesions in each pair.
This may explain why adding the losses during training produced no detected preservation benefit once IO was applied.
The comparison does not test what lesion information the registration network learns, and the segmentation model cannot by itself explain the Dice advantage of IO-only placement: both IO variants use the same predicted masks.

\subsection{Limits of Lesion Preservation}
Three mechanisms must be distinguished.
First, incomplete lesion-mask coverage limits how far IO can reduce image-level MTV and TLG change.
Second, image-level aggregation allows growth and shrinkage of different lesions to cancel.
Third, the soft quantities optimized by the losses differ from the nearest-neighbor quantities used for evaluation.
The contrast between near-zero volume change on the predicted masks and substantial change on the provided masks shows that IO can satisfy its objective without controlling the evaluation endpoint.
Missed lesions were the largest measured contributor to the residual, although they represented only 6\,\% of lesion volume; covered lesions also contributed, so incomplete coverage is not the sole explanation.
Their weight comes from patients with little tumor volume because the metric is relative per image and averaged over patients.
In the third of patients with the smallest lesion volume, missed lesions made up 33\,\% of the volume, compared with 23\,\% and 3\,\% in the other thirds.
This group caused 72\,\% of the missed-lesion contribution.
The post-hoc recall-biased mask increased lesion detection but sharply reduced precision and did not improve measured preservation (Sec.~\ref{sect:res_detection}).
The additional false-positive regions may dilute the preservation constraints over voxels outside the lesions being evaluated, so this experiment does not show that recall gains are ineffective when precision can be retained.
Conversely, the diagnostic run with provided lesion masks nearly eliminated image-level MTV change while improving alignment.
Together, these experiments identify mask quality as a major limit of image-level preservation, but the remaining TLG change shows that segmentation does not explain all residual error.

Because IO meets its objective on the predicted masks already at small weights, additional PET weight mainly costs alignment.
The post-hoc sweep shows that the original validation grid did not sample the low-weight plateau, but a test-set observation cannot define a replacement operating point.
A denser low-weight sweep on validation data followed by independent confirmation would be required before changing the selected weight.

Mask coverage does not explain the second limit: aggregate MTV and TLG changes hide how individual lesions are deformed.
Small lesions are especially sensitive because gaining or losing one or two voxels is already a large relative change.
The location-specific effects were consistent with the losses: rigidity acted mainly on bone-associated lesions, whereas the PET losses acted in both locations.
Nevertheless, IO with the losses did not reduce per-lesion change below the network output, even with provided lesion masks.
The PET losses constrain the total volume and uptake of an image, which can remain nearly unchanged while single lesions grow or shrink.
Indeed, provided masks reduced image-level MTV change to 0.1\,\%, while the median per-lesion change remained 13.5\,\%.

The tested per-lesion terms did not solve this problem on the test set.
They leave small and additional components unconstrained, depend on predicted components that may miss, merge, or split lesions, and use a soft interpolation surrogate that differs from the evaluation.
Even the diagnostic with provided masks did not lower the median per-lesion change.
An effective per-lesion objective may therefore need to include small lesions and more closely match the nearest-neighbor evaluation.

The leave-one-out experiment exposes the third mechanism: the optimized surrogate does not always track the evaluated endpoint.
The Jacobian terms supported the measured preservation, whereas removing either direct measurement term improved evaluated TLG change.
Both terms act on the soft, linearly warped mask, whose change differs from the nearest-neighbor change that is evaluated.
This is one possible explanation for the result, but the experiment does not show that interpolation mismatch caused it.
We retained all four terms because this leave-one-out analysis used the test set and the configuration had been fixed beforehand.
A closer surrogate or a reduced set of terms would require selection on validation data and confirmation on independent data.

\subsection{Interpreting the Evaluation}
The significance-based ranking favored accuracy because alignment differences were more consistent across patients than differences in MTV and TLG.
Our selection rule instead considered preservation among candidates within a fixed Dice tolerance (Sec.~\ref{sect:tuning}).
This makes the trade-off explicit, but it does not establish which balance is preferable for a clinical task.
The MTV and TLG endpoints compare the same follow-up acquisition before and after warping, so they isolate change introduced by registration rather than biological change between examinations.
This study does not define or validate a threshold at which such change alters lesion tracking, response classification, or another clinical decision.
The reported differences should therefore be interpreted as technical fidelity measures rather than demonstrated clinical benefit.
Because image-level changes allow cancellation between lesions, per-lesion changes should accompany them when the intended application follows individual lesions.

Both measured alignment and preservation also depend on the labels: IO fits predicted masks more closely than the independent evaluation labels, and missed lesions receive no direct constraint.
Better lesion masks can therefore improve the reported PET metrics, while organ-mask quality may affect measured alignment.
Label overlap and MTV/TLG change remain proxies for whether registration supports lesion tracking, response assessment, or dose accumulation.

The baseline comparison further shows why deformation regularity must remain a separate endpoint.
The ranking selected NiftyReg's least-regularized setting because accuracy separated its settings more strongly than folding, but that choice produced threshold exceedances on new patients.
A composite rank can therefore obscure localized failures even when its components include a regularity measure.

\subsection{Bone Rigidity}
Finite-difference rigidity penalties face a trade-off at bone boundaries: their stencils also constrain surrounding tissue, whereas eroding the masks to avoid this leaves most of the low-resolution skeleton unconstrained.
The per-bone rigid fit avoids both, because it compares each bone's displacement with its best rigid motion and needs no derivatives.
It produced the lowest within-bone distance distortion among the tested penalties while maintaining soft-tissue alignment.
The cost of rigid bones is lower bone Dice, possibly because a rigid bone cannot follow differences between the automatic bone labels of the two examinations as closely as a freely deforming one.
Whether this lower overlap reflects worse alignment or an inability of a rigid bone to follow inconsistencies between the automatic bone labels cannot be decided from label overlap alone.
The lower within-bone distance distortion shows better preservation of bone shape, but it does not determine whether the bone is placed correctly.

Although about two thirds of the lesion voxels lie on bone labels, adding rigidity on top of the PET losses did not measurably improve lesion volume or uptake preservation in this comparison.
Rigidity alone did improve particular PET endpoints in Fig.~\ref{fig:q2}: it reduced network-output $|\Delta\mathrm{MTV}|$ and IO $|\Delta\mathrm{TLG}|$ relative to the corresponding unconstrained variants.
Three factors may contribute to the absence of a detected additional benefit on top of the PET losses.
First, the PET losses already constrain the predicted lesions.
Second, the residual displacement from a rigid fit is still large enough to move boundary voxels of lesions that span only a few voxels.
Third, the image-level metrics are dominated by missed lesions, by cancellation between lesions, and by a few patients with large changes (Sec.~\ref{sect:res_detection}).
The per-bone rigid fit therefore reduces measured within-bone distance distortion, with a measured cost in bone Dice.
The present measures do not establish whether it improves bone placement, and a further PET-preservation benefit may require bones that are rigid to within a fraction of a voxel.

\subsection{Limitations}
The study uses a single cohort with 28 test patients, so small differences, in particular in MTV and TLG, could not be resolved, and generalization to other scanners and tracers is untested.
The images were resampled to about $2.7\times2.7\times3.3$~mm, at which many lesions consist of few voxels and small lesions are dominated by discretization.
The organ labels used for evaluation are themselves automatic (TotalSegmentator), and the lesion labels used by IO are predictions with a Dice of about 0.5, which, as shown above, limits preservation in IO.
All patients, including the test patients, were part of the challenge data with which the earlier version of the method was developed; the test patients were not used for any decision in the present study.
We studied one network architecture, and we measured the change caused by registration, not its effect on clinical decisions such as response assessment.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sect:conclusion}

For similar whole-body PSMA PET/CT data, loss-guided IO is a reasonable choice when predicted masks and per-pair runtime are available and additional anatomical alignment is desired, but the present results do not establish improved PET preservation over network output.
Mask coverage, cancellation within image totals, and loss--evaluation mismatch limit the current preservation strategy, so image-level MTV and TLG change should be complemented by per-lesion analysis.
The per-bone rigid fit improves measured preservation of internal bone shape, but neither correct bone placement nor additional PET preservation on top of the PET losses was established.
Validation on downstream tasks such as lesion tracking and response assessment is needed to determine whether these technical differences are clinically consequential.
